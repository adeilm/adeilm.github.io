A local AI that explains security alerts: 63% less time to understand them

Every day, a company's security tools raise hundreds of alarms. "Someone tried 50 passwords on a server." "A program changed a system file." A person has to read each alarm and decide: is this a real attack, or a false alarm? It is slow and tiring work.

For our end-of-year university project, my teammate Yessine Abdelmaksoud and I gave that person an AI assistant. The analyst clicks an alarm, and the assistant explains it in plain words, says how serious it looks, and suggests what to check next.

The project in 30 seconds

What
An AI assistant that explains security alerts to the person who handles them.
Where
A small security lab of five virtual machines in our faculty's datacenter.
How
A free AI model (Qwen3 14B) running on our own machine, so no data leaves the lab.
Result
Time to understand an alert: 342 s → 127 s, in a small test (15 alerts, 2 students).
Team
Two students: Yessine Abdelmaksoud and me.

Words you'll see

SOC
Security Operations Center: the team that watches a company's systems for attacks.
Alert
An automatic warning: "this looks suspicious". Many alerts are false alarms.
Wazuh, Suricata
Free tools that raise alerts. Wazuh watches computers. Suricata watches network traffic.
LLM
Large language model: the kind of AI behind ChatGPT. Ours runs on our own machine.
MITRE ATT&CK
A public list of attacker techniques, each with an ID. T1110 means "guessing passwords".

The problem: too many alarms

The tools in a SOC raise alerts all day, and someone has to check each one. When Oxford researchers interviewed SOC analysts, they confirmed high false-positive rates that require manual validation, which "can cause alarm burnout".

Reading one alert is slow, too. The analyst sees a rule number, IP addresses and codes. To understand the story, they search for related events by hand.

We did not try to catch new attacks. We tried to make each alert faster to understand.

Our idea: an assistant that reads first

We added one helper between the alarm and the person. It never replaces the analyst: it reads first, then explains.

AttackSomeone tries to break in. In our lab, we did it on purpose.
triggers
Alarm toolsWazuh and Suricata notice it and raise an alert.
on click
AI assistantGathers related events and explains the alert.
explains
AnalystReads the explanation and decides what to do.

For each alert, the assistant answers three questions:

What happened?

A short summary, how serious it looks, and the attack stage from MITRE ATT&CK.

How do I check?

One to three ready-made searches for KibanaThe screen where analysts search and read alerts. KQL is its search language..

What should I do?

One action from a fixed list of ten, with reasons and a confidence score.

Follow one alert: a password-guessing attack

Here is one of our test attacks, step by step. Values are illustrative, and our real outputs were in French, so I've translated.

  1. The attack. A script tries many passwords for root, the administrator account, on our test server vm-endp1. This is called a brute-force attack.
  2. The alarms. Suricata sees the attack in the network traffic. 35 seconds later, Wazuh sees the failed logins on the server and raises alert 5763.
  3. The click. The analyst opens the alert. Our service first gathers related events: same IP address, same machine or same user, in a short time window. One alert becomes a small story with a timeline.
  4. The answer. The AI reads that story and explains it:

Without AI: the raw alert

rule.id       5763
rule.mitre    T1110
agent.name    vm-endp1
data.srcip    10.0.0.66
data.dstuser  root

The analyst must look up the rule, the IP and the technique, then search for related events.

With our assistant

"Repeated failed SSH logins as root on vm-endp1 from 10.0.0.66, also flagged by Suricata. Looks like automated password guessing. No successful login in the context: check for an accepted login from this IP."

Severity: highStage: credential accessT1110.001: password guessing

The AI added what the raw alert didn't say: the exact technique, whether the attack seems to have worked, and what to check next.

Inside the lab

Here is the full setup. Five Ubuntu virtual machines ran in our faculty's datacenter (on CloudStack/KVM):

  • Endpoint: the test server we attacked. A Wazuh agent on it reports what happens.
  • Wazuh manager: collects those reports and raises alerts about the computer.
  • Suricata: watches the network traffic and raises alerts about it.
  • Elastic Stack: stores all alerts (Elasticsearch) and shows them (Kibana).
  • AI machine: runs the model (Qwen3 14B in Ollama), our service (FastAPI) and a small web page for the analyst.

Scroll slowly: an alert travels through the diagram, from detection to the analyst.

Three rules kept the AI part safe:

  1. It runs only when asked. It works when the analyst clicks, not on every alert.
  2. It writes in its own place. Its answers go to a separate indexA storage space in Elasticsearch, like a separate table in a database., never into the real alerts.
  3. It cannot act. It cannot block anything or change a rule. If the AI is down, the SOC works as before.

Did it help?

We ran a small, controlled test. Two student analysts handled 15 alerts twice: once as the raw alert in Kibana, once in our tool. We stopped the timer when they could say what was happening and what they would do.

342 sRaw alert in Kibana
127 sWith the AI assistant
↓ 63%Mean time to understand an alert

15 alerts · 2 student analysts · controlled test

That is a good sign, not proof:

  • The test was tiny, and the analysts were students. Experienced analysts may gain less.
  • We chose the alerts, from attacks we ran ourselves. Real queues are full of false alarms.
  • We measured speed, not correctness. An AI can be confident and wrong. NIST calls this confabulation and warns that people tend to trust automation too much.

Larger studies of Microsoft's Security Copilot point the same way, in randomized trials and in live operations. I compare the direction, not the numbers.

What I learned

The AI call was the easy part. Most of the work, and most of the value, was around it: collecting context, forcing a strict answer format, and making sure the AI can't act without a person.

Next, I would run a bigger, blind test that checks whether decisions were right, attack the inputs on purpose (see "log text" below), and add a "useful / not useful" button to collect feedback.

For engineers

The parts below are for people who build these systems. Open the ones you need.

Forcing a valid answer: JSON schema + validation

Our first version used Ollama's format: "json", and every response failed validation: the model returned {}, valid JSON but empty. Passing the JSON schema itself as format fixed that.

But constrained decoding can skip schema keywords it doesn't support (silently, in llama.cpp's case). So Ollama gets only the structure, and jsonschema checks the rest, like text lengths and the ATT&CK ID pattern:

payload = {
    "model": "qwen3:14b",
    "prompt": prompt,                 # instructions + schema + alert JSON
    "format": structural_schema,      # constrains generation
    "think": False,
    "options": {"temperature": 0.2},
}
result = json.loads(ollama_reply["response"])
errors = [e.message for e in validator.iter_errors(result)]  # full schema

We retry once. If that fails too, the result is saved with validated: false, so the analyst sees that the AI failed.

Closed lists instead of open questions

The attack phase comes from a fixed list, and the remediation is one of ten fixed action IDs, such as block_source_ip. The model picks; it never writes commands, and nothing runs automatically, not even the KQL queries.

The prompt also asks for a time filter on each query, but code doesn't check that yet. Closed lists turn "is this answer good?" into "is this answer allowed?", which code can check.

Treating log text as hostile (prompt injection)

Attacker-written text, like the fake User-Agent in one of our scenarios, lands in the alert and then in the prompt. This is indirect prompt injection, which OWASP ranks first (LLM01:2025) among LLM risks.

So alert data sits in its own JSON block that the model must never take instructions from, and indicators of compromise come only from structured fields. With closed lists and no tools, an injection can at worst pick a wrong option. We didn't red-team this, and a line in a prompt is not a security boundary.

Why the model runs locally

Alerts are full of usernames, hostnames and internal IPs, so nothing goes to a third-party API. The open-weights Qwen3 14B runs in Ollama on 127.0.0.1, reachable only by the FastAPI service on the same VM. The cost is speed: on a CPU-only VM, you notice the wait.

How we tested, and the ATT&CK caveat

We automated five end-to-end attack scenarios: SSH brute force, a port scan, a change to /etc/passwd, an ICMP flood and a suspicious User-Agent. Each script runs the attack, waits for detection and checks that all three answers pass validation.

In the timed test, we alternated which version each analyst saw first.

ATT&CK mappings are suggestions. A port scan could be T1046 (Discovery) or T1595 (Reconnaissance), depending on whether the attacker is already inside. And traditional ML models beat ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini at labeling Snort rules with ATT&CK techniques. Next, I would check IDs against MITRE's STIX data, not just their format.
